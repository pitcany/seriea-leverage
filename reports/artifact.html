Where this came from

A paper that stopped one step short

In 2016 I wrote Soccer Match Prediction in the Serie A, which was accepted for publication by the International Journal of Computer Applications in 2018. It applied SVMs, AdaBoost and logistic regression to ten seasons and reported 53.0% three-way accuracy — comparing that favourably against betting markets at 55.3%.

Two things were wrong with that comparison, and both are instructive.

The benchmark was uniform guessing at 33%. But home advantage alone gets you to the base rate, which over this corpus is 40.6% — so roughly seven points of the apparent edge were never an edge at all. And the model was fed the bookmaker’s odds as features, then congratulated for approaching the bookmaker’s accuracy. A model handed the market’s own forecast should at minimum match it. Scoring below it means the pipeline is destroying information, not adding any.

The paper noticed the symptom — “the betting odds did not impact predictions very much” — and misread it as the odds being uninformative.

I

What the original numbers said

Before rebuilding anything, I recomputed the 2016 results from the paper’s own confusion matrices. Both total 218 matches, against a stated test set of 320 — the difference is early-season fixtures dropped for insufficient history, never reconciled in the text.

Away wins were 60 / 218 = 27.5% of the test set.
So “never predict an away win” scores 72.5%.

Reported away accuracy — Gaussian SVM 72.9 · Linear SVM 69.7 · AdaBoost 68.4 · Logistic 45.9

Not one of them beat the constant.

The home side of that table is sharper still. Home wins were 92/218, so never predicting a home win scores 126/218 = 57.80%. The paper reports Linear SVM home accuracy of 57.8. That is not a coincidence to three significant figures: the classifier almost certainly never predicted a single home win.

Two smaller things fell out of the same arithmetic. Table 4.2 is captioned “Linear SVM” but its diagonal — 114/218 = 52.3% — identifies it as AdaBoost. And with 218 matches the standard error on an accuracy near one half is ±3.4 points, so the paper’s entire reported spread of 47.2% to 53.0% sits inside two standard errors. The model ranking it drew was noise.

II

The rebuild

Nineteen complete seasons, 2007–08 through 2025–26: 7,220 matches. Bookmaker prices converted to probabilities by Shin’s method rather than proportional normalisation, which leaves longshots systematically over-priced. A Dixon–Coles bivariate Poisson replaces the classifiers — attack and defence ratings per club, a dependence correction on the four low-scoring lines, and exponential time decay.

The decay rate is the direct analogue of the original paper’s e−i form weighting, which was asserted rather than fitted. That weighting was doubly wrong: it normalised by window length instead of by the sum of weights, and it decayed so fast that a nominal five-match window carried an effective sample size of about 2.1 matches. Here the rate is chosen on a validation period and nothing else.

Everything is scored walk-forward: fit on the past, predict the next fortnight, advance, refit. The original used five-fold cross-validation on time-ordered data, which lets a fold containing March inform a prediction about January. Hyperparameters, the pooling weight and the calibration temperature were all fixed on 2013–19. The 2019–26 test period was scored once.

III

The verdict

The result

In a logarithmic pool with the market, the optimal weight on the structural model is 0.000.

Not small. Zero — on the validation period, and still zero if you cheat and fit it on the test set. Log loss rises monotonically the moment any weight moves onto the model.

So the honest answer to the question the 2016 paper was really asking is no. A goals-based structural model, fitted carefully on nineteen seasons with a tuned decay and a proper likelihood, carries no information a de-vigged closing price has not already absorbed. Against the market it is worse by +0.0068 RPS [+0.0047, +0.0090] — a small gap, but a decisively non-zero one.

2016 study accuracy 53.0% Ten seasons, MATLAB, SVM with market odds as features.
This rebuild 53.4% Nineteen seasons, tuned decay, proper likelihood, walk-forward.
The market 54.8% Pinnacle closing, margin removed. Still ahead of both.

Nine extra seasons and a decade of better method bought 0.4 accuracy points — well inside noise. That is worth saying plainly rather than burying: three-way match accuracy is near its ceiling, and a team that keeps grinding on it is optimising a number that has stopped moving.

One nuance the headline hides. The model is better calibrated than the market on home wins — calibration slope 0.993 against the market’s 1.103 — while being clearly less sharp. Fitted recalibration temperature was 0.99, meaning the model needed essentially no correction. The market’s advantage is information, not honesty. Those are different failures and they need different fixes.

IV

What is left, and why a club wants it

If you cannot beat the market on match outcomes, stop trying to sell match outcomes. A club does not need a marginally worse copy of a number it can read for free. It needs that number aggregated into a decision.

Run the calibrated forecasts forward: simulate the rest of the season twenty thousand times, sampling full scorelines so goal difference resolves itself, and read off the probability of the things a club is actually managing — Europe, survival, the title. Then ask, for each remaining fixture, how far the result moves that probability. That difference is leverage, and it is the chart at the top of this page.

The ordering is not what instinct says. The six-pointers against fellow strugglers dominate; the fixtures against the top six barely move survival either way, because the model already expects defeat in both branches and has priced it. Losing at home to Inter costs Fiorentina about 3.3 points of survival probability. Losing away at Lecce costs 7.4.

That is a rotation argument stated in probability rather than in instinct: the Inter fixture is where a tiring first-choice midfielder is cheapest to rest, and Lecce away is where he is most expensive. No 1X2 forecast tells you that on its own.

Projected final points 41.4 From 1 March, with twelve fixtures unplayed.
Actual 42 Fiorentina finished 15th. Modelled survival probability was 96.4%.

The projection landing within 0.6 points of the real total is a single draw from a distribution and I would not over-read it. It does establish that the season aggregation is not obviously broken, which is the minimum bar before anyone acts on a leverage ranking.

—

What this does not do

  • No player-level information. No injuries, suspensions, rest days or transfers. This is the largest gap against what a club holds internally, and the most likely place a real edge over a market price still exists.
  • No expected goals. Goal-based strength is noisier than xG-based strength. This is the first thing I would add.
  • The market benchmark is a closing price, which absorbs late team news the model never sees. Some of that 0.0068 RPS gap is information asymmetry rather than modelling.
  • Leverage is single-objective. It conditions on one position band at a time. A club weighing survival against a cup run needs a utility, not a probability swing.
  • Tiebreakers are simplified to points, then goal difference, then goals scored. Serie A resolves equal points on head-to-head first.
